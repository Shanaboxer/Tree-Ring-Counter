How the count is made. The path is resampled with cubic interpolation at quarter-pixel spacing, then flattened against a Gaussian local baseline and local spread, so a faint 3 px ring near the bark is judged on the same scale as a bold 12 px ring near the pith. Every local minimum then becomes a candidate boundary, scored by prominence — how far the signal has to climb to escape it on both sides — and the sensitivity slider is the cut-off on that score. A Morlet wavelet ridge still runs, but only to estimate how wide the rings are locally; it sets the baseline window and the minimum spacing between marks, and it no longer does the counting. The fan repeats the measurement on nearby angles; the spread across them is the quoted uncertainty.

The limit that no setting can move. A ring has to be about three pixels wide in your source photograph before its dark boundary survives as a dip in the signal at all. Below roughly two pixels per ring the boundaries have already been averaged away by the camera and the JPEG encoder, and there is nothing left in the file to find. If a band reads as smooth wood at 8× zoom, no sensitivity setting will recover it — the fix is a larger photograph of that band, ideally of a sanded face.

What it still cannot decide for you. A sharp minimum can be a true annual boundary or an intra-annual density fluctuation — a false ring laid down in a dry spell. No photograph settles that, which is why the marks are editable. And the first two or three years sit in a handful of pixels around the pith, where no algorithm can separate them; if your line starts there, count those by eye.
